Dynamic shapes not only change the amount of work in an operator, but also
alter the optimal configuration of kernel parameters.
We use a GEMM-based fused
operator to demonstrate this effect.  The operator keeps the reduction and
output-channel dimensions fixed at $K=768$ and $N=2304$, while the dynamic token dimension $M$ varies from 24 to 256.  For each shape, we
exhaustively evaluate 154 valid configurations represented as tuples $(\texttt{BLOCK\_M}, \texttt{BLOCK\_N}, \texttt{BLOCK\_K}, \texttt{num\_warps}, and \texttt{num\_stages})$.

Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-config-heatmap} shows that the optimal
configuration is not stable across shapes.  In the heatmap, each cell reports
latency normalized to that of the optimal configuration, and the star
marks the per-shape optimum.  Among the eight tested shapes, the per-shape
optimum comes from seven different configurations.  For example, the optimal configuration changes from
$(32,64,64,2,4)$ at $M=24$, to $(64,64,32,4,5)$ at $M=100$, and to
$(32,64,32,2,5)$ at $M=256$.  Such variation occurs because the tiling paradigm
induces trade-offs under different $M$ values: small shapes require
enough parallel tasks to occupy the GPU, while larger shapes can benefit more
from data reuse, pipeline depth, and reduced scheduling overhead.
%Consequently, a configuration that is close to optimal for one shape may be

Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-static-loss} quantifies the consequence of adopting a
single configuration.  The static baseline selects the optimal
configuration at the largest shape ($M=256$) and reuses it for all shapes.  This
baseline incurs an average latency increase of 11.6\%, rising to 52.9\%
at $M=100$.  These results indicate that there is no one-size-fits-all
configuration for dynamic-shape fused operators.  At the same time,
exhaustively tuning every possible runtime shape is too expensive for
end-to-end inference.  This motivates a shape-adaptive auto-tuning mechanism that
%configuration selection
can quickly identify a high-performance configuration for each unseen
shape at runtime. %without running full autotuning.

\label{sec:bottleneck}

The variation in optimal configurations is rooted in hardware-level bottleneck
shifts.  To further understand this effect, we profile the same GEMM-based
fused operator with a wider range of dynamic shapes, where $M$ increases from
24 to 4096 while $N$ and $K$ remain fixed.  We compare two distinct
configurations: Config-A uses a larger tile
$(128,128,32)$, while Config-B uses a smaller tile $(32,32,64)$. We collect NCU metrics including achieved SM occupancy, L2 cache hit rate, and DRAM throughput.

Figure~\ref{fig:motivation-bottleneck} shows that the preferred configuration changes with the hardware behavior. The normalized latency is computed against the faster of the two configurations at each shape. At small shapes, Config-B is up to 2.5$\times$ faster than Config-A because Config-A' large tile creates too few thread blocks to keep the GPU busy, leading to low occupancy. In contrast, Config-B generates more independent tiles and exhibits high thread block-level parallelism.

As $M$ increases, Config-A becomes faster from $M=256$ onward, reaching 2.5$\times$ faster than Config-B at $M=4096$.  Although Config-B maintains high occupancy, Config-A obtains increasingly better data reuse: its L2 cache hit rate grows from 42.8\% at $M=24$ to 92.7\% at $M=4096$. Meanwhile, the DRAM throughput of Config-B drops from 25.5\% to 4.3\%, indicating that the smaller tile struggles with repeated memory traffic and scheduling overhead. %Therefore, the optimization objective moves from exposing enough parallelism to improving data reuse and execution efficiency.

This bottleneck shift explains why a static configuration cannot cover all shapes at runtime.  The optimal configuration is determined not only by the operator implementation, 
but also by how the tiled computations are mapped to hardware resources.
